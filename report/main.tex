\noindent
Parts that a particle beam hits on purpose, such as targets and collimators, are designed in two
steps: a Monte Carlo code such as FLUKA~\cite{fluka2005} computes where the beam deposits its energy,
then a finite-element code computes the resulting temperature and thermal stress. tmfem rebuilds the
second step from scratch in C++17, without any finite-element library, and checks every capability
with an automated test against an analytical solution or a reference value.

\section{Methods}

tmfem solves transient heat conduction and diffusion, $\rho c\,\dot T = \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + q$,
in 1D and 2D with the $\theta$-method (backward Euler or Crank--Nicolson). It solves 2D linear
elasticity in plane stress and plane strain with three-node (T3) and six-node (T6) triangles, using
the stiffness matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf K = \sum_e \int_{\Omega_e} \mathbf B^T \mathbf D\, \mathbf B \,\mathrm dA .
\end{equation}
Plasticity follows the von Mises model with isotropic hardening, integrated by the radial return
algorithm with a consistent tangent~\cite{simo_hughes}, and solved with Newton's method. The
temperature field enters the mechanical problem as a thermal strain $\alpha(T - T_\text{ref})$.
Meshes are generated with Gmsh~\cite{gmsh}, and linear systems are solved with Eigen.

\section{Validation}

The benchmarks come from lab exercises in diffusion, heat transfer and mechanics of materials
(M.~R.~Kabiri, ENSAM Mekn\`es), from textbook solutions~\cite{crank,timoshenko_goodier}, and from an
Abaqus simulation. Every reference value was recomputed independently before being used as a test
target. Table~\ref{tab:results} summarises the main results.

The stress concentration at the hole matches the exact value of 3 to 0.01\%, and the stresses around
it match Kirsch's solution (Figure~\ref{fig:kirsch}). The observed convergence orders match theory
for both element types. The plasticity solver reproduces the Abaqus strains of the tensile test, and
the quadratic convergence of Newton's method confirms that the tangent is consistent. When the plate
with a hole is loaded to 140 MPa, the material yields at the edge of the hole and a plastic zone of
0.17 times the hole radius forms. The edge strain is 11\% below the estimate of Neuber's
rule~\cite{neuber1961}, which is known to overestimate notch strains.

\section{Demonstration: a beam-heated block}

As a demonstration, a Gaussian beam deposits 1 kJ per metre in 10~$\mu$s into a long block of copper
or graphite, and tmfem computes the temperature and the thermal stress (Figure~\ref{fig:demo}). For a
1 mm beam, graphite heats up 2.65 times more than copper (123 K against 46.2 K) but carries 20 times
less stress (3.7 against 74.3 MPa). Copper exceeds its yield stress for beams of 1 mm or smaller,
while graphite stays far below its strength. This illustrates why graphite is used to absorb beams.

\section{Conclusion}

tmfem implements the main methods behind a thermal-stress analysis of a beam-heated component, and
each of them is confirmed by an automated test against an independent answer: analytical solutions,
textbook results, and an Abaqus reference.
